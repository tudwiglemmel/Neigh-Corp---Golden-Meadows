\section{Introduction}
This section is to give a general introduction to the company, including a description of its
main product offerings, markets, and strategy.

Neigh Corp is a small game company aiming to create its first horse-themed game.
The product is an MMORPG with "gacha mechanics", a video game system in which the player spends in-game currency to receive a random in-game item, that combines various semi-realistic, real-life horse-related activities into a single game. The game is designed to be refreshing, immersive, and modern, and allows the player to roleplay as a horse trainer, enjoy riding in an open world, take part in competitions, and gather and sell horses and horse equipment. The game will have a lively community active in the game forum.

The desired customer base consists mostly of teenagers and young adults who are interested in horses. Neigh Corp welcomes experienced gamers as well as players who are completely new to gaming.

The primary sources of income are a recurring premium membership subscription that is offered to players, as well as in-game cosmetic purchases and microtransactions for in-game advantages.

\subsection{VDML Diagram Explained}
Seen above is the VDML diagram designed for Golden Meadows, featuring Neigh Corp as the central agent involved in the majority of value exchange processes. The diagram provides a comprehensive overview of the majority of actors involved in value propositions directly affecting the game.

The inclusion of individual agents was decided based on the previously-designed goal model as well as the actors typically engaged in the game development as well as MMO game maintenance processes. Each of the identified agents' involvements in a value proposition is briefly described below, as well as the agent itself where its name is not self-explanatory.

\subsubsection{Value Propositions between External Agents}

Several actors included on the VDML diagram are engaged in value exchanges without Neigh Corp's direct involvement, namely:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Free-to-play players as well as pay-to-play players and game forum}
  
    The \textit{Free-to-Play Players} actor, representing users of \textit{Golden Meadows} who do not conduct any in-game purchases, is engaged in a value exchange with \textit{Game Forum}, representing the online community where players can participate in conversations via forum posts. \textit{Pay-to-Play Players}, who are denoted as users actively purchasing in-game items and premium benefits, are, too, engaged in an identical value exchange with the exact same VPs. The following value propositions are involved in this relationship:
  
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item Community VP and Anticipation VP - given to the \textit{Players};
    \item Discussion Activity VP - given to the \textit{Game Forum}.
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Game influencers and Free-to-Play as well as Pay-to-Play Players}

    The \textit{Free-to-Play} and \textit{Pay-to-Play Player} actors are also engaged in another value exchange with the \textit{Game Influencers} - an actor representing agents such as popular players, You-Tubers, as well as streamers contributing to the game's popularity. The value propositions are:

    \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Watch Time VP} - given to the \textit{Influencers};
    \item \textit{Entertainment VP} - given to the \textit{Players}.
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Game Influencers and Online Advertisers}

    The value exchange between \textbf{Game Influencers} and \textbf{Online Advertisers} involves the following value propositions:

    \begin{enumerate}
    \item Money VP - given to \textit{Game Influencers};
    \item Third-party Brand Exposure/Review VP - given to the \textit{Online Advertisers}.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}


\subsubsection{Value Propositions involving Neigh Corp}

The majority of value exchange relationships depicted on the VDML diagram include Neigh Corp, described below:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pay-to-play players}

    The exchange between pay-to-play players and Neigh Corp involves \textit{Money VP}, given to Neigh Corp, as well as \textit{Premium Game Access VP}, given to the pay-to-play players.

   \item \textbf{Game Forum}

   The \textit{Game Forum} agent receives the \textit{Money VP} from Neigh Corp in exchange for \textit{User Feedback VP}.

    \item \textbf{Free-to-play players}

    Neigh Corp receives \textit{Feedback VP} from the Players agent, defined on the diagram seen above, in exchange for \textit{Base Game Access VP}.

    \item \textbf{Beta testers}

    \textit{Beta Testers} receive \textit{Early Access VP}, and in turn, Neigh Corp receives \textit{User Feedback VP}. Both value propositions are given concrete definitions on the image seen above, below the VDML diagram.

    \item \textbf{Game Influencers}

    \textit{Game Influencers} receive \textit{Premium Game Access VP} in exchange for \textit{Advertisement VP}.

    \item \textbf{Online Advertisers}

    \textit{Online Advertisers} are engaged in a value exchange with Neigh Corp, receiving the \textit{Online Advertiser In-game exposure VP}. The actor, in turn, gives \textit{Money VP} as well as \textit{Anticipation VP}.

    \item \textbf{Real-world brands}

    \textit{Real-world brands} provide Neigh Corp with the \textit{Money VP}. The actor receives \textit{Real world competitions In-game exposure VP} (see definition).

    The \textit{Real-world brands} actor represents real-life companies wishing to engage in a partnership with Neigh Corp, provided with ad placement within the game in exchange for a monthly or one-time fee, decided on a case-by-case basis and the duration of ad placement.

    \item \textbf{Third-party authorized cosmetics creators on marketplace}

    The actor represents individual users or groups of people engaged in the development of in-game cosmetics. In exchange for the provided \textit{In-Game Content VP}, the actor receives \textit{Money VP} from Neigh Corp.

    \item \textbf{Penetration testers}

    Penetration testers receive \textit{Money VP} from Neigh Corp in exchange for conducting penetration tests in order to identify potential security vulnerabilities within the company's infrastructure, represented by the \textit{Pen-Test Results VP}.

    \item  \textbf{Outsourced Development Contractor}

    Third-party developers hired by Neigh Corp for various development tasks are represented by this agent, receiving the \textit{Money VP} in exchange for \textit{Game Development VP}.

    \item \textbf{Third-Party AI Vendor}

    \textbf{Third-Party AI Vendors} is an agent representing companies providing Neigh Corp with a miscellaneous set of AI tools, for instance, copilots, agents, or data gathering software. The provided \textit{AI Services VP} is exchanged for \textit{Money VP}.

    \item \textbf{Advertising Company}

    The advertising company actor receives \textit{Money VP} from Neigh Corp in exchange for the advertising services the agent provides, represented by the \textit{Advertisement VP}.

    \item \textbf{Game Vendor}

    The \textit{Game Vendor} agent, denoting a game marketplace providing companies with a platform to sell their product on, exchanges \textit{Anticipation VP} for \textit{Money VP}, anticipation in this context meaning teasers being released ahead of the game's or its update's release.

    \item \textbf{Server Provider}

    The \textit{Server Provider} agent gives Neigh Corp the \textit{Server Hosting VP}, in turn receiving \textit{Money VP}.

    \item \textbf{Insurance Company}

    \textit{Insurance Company} provides Neigh Corp with the \textit{Insurances VP}, which represents one or more active insurance agreements. Neigh Corp pays for said insurance with the \textit{Money VP}.

    \item \textbf{Employees}

    The agent representing \textit{Employees} receives \textit{Money VP} from the company in exchange for \textit{Labour VP}.

    \item \textbf{Payment Provider}

    The agent representing \textit{Payment Providers} receives \textit{Money VP} from the company in exchange for \textit{Payment Service VP}.
    

\end{enumerate}

The first level at the bottom, named "Learning \& Growth", contains the foundational technologies, tools, and infrastructure used in the company. This level supports the one above it, "Internal Business Processes", which lists the internal development methods and operational processes the company uses to create value. The "Customer" perspective contains the most elements and describes how these internal processes create differentiated value and loyalty for the customer, and the final "Financial" level at the top shows how all these elements combine to fulfil the most important monetary goals in the company, such as profit and recurring revenue.

The goal model was created with the previously shown \autoref{fig:VDML_diagram} diagram in mind and in accordance with Porter's five forces \cite{quickmba_porter} and Porter's generic strategies. Each of Porter's five forces is listed below with an analysis of which parts of the diagram belong to it, which generic strategy is used to combat its force, and the reasoning behind their Strategy Maps classification. The overarching approach for Golden Meadows is differentiation focus, as the target customer group for this game is rather niche.

\textbf{Competitive rivalry:}
This force measures how many competing products there are on the market. A large number of competitors results in a greater force. Influencers 14 and 16 measure this force. They indicate that there is increasing competition from Chinese gaming companies and that there is one established competitor with high market share and a high cost of switching. This makes the competitive rivalry force comparatively strong, since the potential for new competitors emerging has increased, and potential customers experience a high cost of switching to Golden Meadows. 

A number of goals and means are in place to combat this force. Means 26 is about making Golden Meadows free to play, as shown in the VDML \autoref{fig:VDML_diagram} with the Free-to-Play Players actor. This makes the game accessible to new users at no cost and therefore lowers the upfront switching cost to zero. Means 26 belongs to the "Customer" perspective, as it directly shapes the value proposition offered to players and supports Goal 5, getting many horse-interested users to play Golden Meadows (operationalised by Objective 4).

Goal 9, establishing a strongly recognizable brand (operationalised by Objective 2), also combats this force by establishing Golden Meadows' individual identity. While competitors can copy graphics, the means that go into this goal, such as collaborating with real-life horse competitions and horse brands (Means 7 and 8, capitalising on Influencer 8) and hiring influencers (Means 9), are harder to replicate. Both Goal 9 and Means 7, 8, and 9 are placed in the "Customer" perspective because they focus on external brand recognition and customer perception. These means and goals relate directly to the VDML Game Influencers and Real-world brands actors that provide Neigh Corp with advertisement and in-game brand exposure. In the Strategy Map, Goal 5 and Goal 9 support Goal 6 (building a large player base in the "Customer" perspective), which in turn drives the top-level "Financial" goal, Goal 1 (to profit from Golden Meadows).

\textbf{Bargaining Power of Buyers:}
This force measures how much power the buyers have. The influencers in the goal model that show weakness in this force are Influencers 11 and 13, which state that the target group is easily convinced to gamble and pay in games. The factors that strengthen this force are Influencers 15 and 21, which state that parents control the purchases of younger players and that pop-up ads can decrease their game enjoyment.

To combat this force, among others, Means 17 is implemented, which presents the customers with an optional, regular subscription for continued premium game access. This can appease the minds of concerned parents with a predictable monthly expense. In the Strategy Map, Means 17, alongside Means 14, 15, 16, and 18 (integrating various in-game currencies, creating premium memberships, offering gacha chances for special edition horses, and giving daily login rewards) belongs to the "Customer" perspective, as these elements define the purchasing options and reward structures experienced by the player. They support Goal 4 (getting users to make in-game payments, operationalised by Objective 3) and Goal 10 (high long-term user retention, operationalised by Objective 5) in the "Customer" perspective, which then flow upward into the "Financial" perspective to fulfil Goal 8 (generating recurring revenue) and Goal 1 (profiting from Golden Meadows). Conversely, Means 22 (showing pop-up ads) belongs to the "Internal Business Process" perspective and is threatened by Influencer 21, as intrusive internal ad implementations reduce player enjoyment. This subscription and payment structure is directly related to the VDML value transfer between Neigh Corp and Pay-to-Play Players. Buyer power is further weakened by the niche approach that differentiates Golden Meadows from competitors. Therefore, customers have few similar alternatives to switch to and their bargaining power is reduced.

\textbf{Bargaining Power of Suppliers:}
This force measures how much power the suppliers have over the company in terms of pricing, platform rules, and technical dependencies. In the goal model, this force is strengthened by Influencer 5, which states that Steam prohibits intrusive advertisements in games, and Influencer 23, which states that cloud infrastructure providers have high pricing power, making server costs high. It is further strengthened by Influencers 25 and 26, which highlight that integrating proprietary AI tools and payment providers creates deep code dependencies and high technical switching costs. On the other hand, the force is slightly weakened by Influencer 4, which notes that Steam allows product placements and collaborations within games.

To address supplier power across the Strategy Map, technological dependencies are handled in the foundational "Learning \& Growth" and "Internal Business Process" perspectives. Means 34 (investing in scalable infrastructure, threatened by Influencer 23) and Means 23 (using third-party AI tools, threatened by Influencer 25) belong to the "Learning \& Growth" perspective because they represent the core IT infrastructure and external software capabilities of the company. Means 34 supports Goal 7 (ensuring the same quality of game mechanics for all) in the "Internal Business Process" perspective, while Means 23 implements Means 21 (using AI for maintenance in "Learning \& Growth") and Means 20 (using AI in development in "Internal Business Process"). Similarly, Means 13 (integrating a multi-device, multi-currency payment provider, threatened by Influencer 26) is classified as an "Internal Business Process" that enables Goal 4 in the "Customer" perspective. Because the Game Vendor's restrictions on intrusive ads (Influencer 5) limit the use of pop-up advertisements (Means 22) on Steam, Neigh Corp complements its ad model with "Customer" perspective initiatives that take advantage of Influencer 4, such as making the game available on game vendors (Means 2) and collaborating with real-life horse competitions and brands (Means 7 and 8). In the VDML diagram, these relationships correspond to the Server Provider, Third-Party AI Vendor, Payment Provider, Game Vendor, and Real-world brands actors. By using AI to lower development costs and establishing steady recurring revenue (Goal 8) in the "Financial" perspective, Neigh Corp is able to absorb the high costs and switching barriers imposed by its technical suppliers.

\textbf{Threat of New Entrants:}
This force measures how easily new competitors can enter the market and compete with Golden Meadows. Influencer 27 strengthens this force by stating that advances in AI and LLMs have decreased the required software-development effort, which lowers the barrier to entry for new studios. Influencer 20 (advances in visual graphics technology) also strengthens this threat against Goal 2, as it raises players' expectations for graphical fidelity and allows new entrants with modern tools to attract attention. Furthermore, Influencer 1 indicates that Steam users and streamers often give a lot of attention to new games from unknown developers; while this is an opportunity for Neigh Corp at launch, it also makes market entry easier for future newcomers.

Neigh Corp combats the threat of new entrants by turning AI into an internal advantage and building complex, niche game mechanics that cannot be quickly replicated. In the Strategy Map, Neigh Corp uses Means 20, 21, and 23 (spanning the "Learning \& Growth" and "Internal Business Process" perspectives) alongside Means 19 (outsourcing non-core mechanic development in the "Internal Business Process" perspective) to reduce its own development costs and directly support Goal 1 in the "Financial" perspective. To defend against rising graphical expectations (Influencer 20) and create a deep product barrier, the company implements several "Internal Business Process" means: making the game visually pleasing (Means 27), developing a horse-focused open world (Means 28), adding world-wide, open-world, and stable competitions (Means 29, 30, and 32), and implementing genetics and breeding mechanics (Means 31 and 33). These are placed in the "Internal Business Process" perspective because they represent core development tasks carried out by Neigh Corp's employees and Outsourced Development Contractors in the VDML. Moving up the Strategy Map, these internal processes support Goal 2 (making Golden Meadows enjoyable to play for the target market, operationalised by Objective 8) in the "Customer" perspective. Simultaneously, Neigh Corp leverages Influencer 1 through "Customer" perspective means: creating early access (Means 1), publishing on game vendors (Means 2), and allowing and encouraging streamers and YouTubers to share gameplay (Means 3, 4, 5, and 6) to achieve Goal 3 (gaining a loyal community, operationalised by Objective 1). This connects to the Beta Testers, Game Vendor, and Game Influencers in the VDML and establishes an early player base before new entrants can establish themselves.

\textbf{Threat of Substitutes:}
This force measures how easily customers can satisfy their needs with alternative products or activities outside of the direct horse-game niche. In the goal model, Influencers 28 and 29 strengthen this force: Influencer 28 states that short-form media dominates the screentime of younger demographics (threatening Goal 10), and Influencer 29 states that casual mobile games offer instant gratification without the learning curve of complex simulation mechanics (threatening Goal 2). However, Influencer 30 states that real-world equestrian hobbies require high financial investments that are inaccessible to many people. This is beneficial for Neigh Corp, as it weakens the threat of the primary real-life substitute and acts as an opportunity for Goal 5.

To capitalise on Influencer 30 and counter Influencer 29, Neigh Corp combines its "Internal Business Process" simulation mechanics (Means 28--33) with the "Customer" perspective Means 26 (making Golden Meadows free to play). Because real-life horse riding is prohibitively expensive, offering an immersive, free-to-play virtual alternative in the "Customer" perspective makes Golden Meadows highly attractive to horse-interested users (Goal 5), serving both Free-to-Play and Pay-to-Play Players in the VDML. To combat the distraction of short-form media (Influencer 28) and secure high long-term user retention (Goal 10 in the "Customer" perspective), Neigh Corp implements social and reward-based means in the "Customer" perspective: implementing a stable/club system (Means 24), organizing community events (Means 25), offering special edition horses (Means 16), and providing daily login rewards (Means 18). These social features relate to the Game Forum actor in the VDML and support Goal 6 (building a large player base) and Goal 10 in the "Customer" perspective, which subsequently secure steady in-game payments (Goal 4) and recurring revenue (Goal 8) in the "Financial" perspective.

\textbf{Generic strategies:}
To combat all five forces in a single, consistent way, Neigh Corp applies a differentiation focus (niche) strategy paired with a grassroots, influencer- and community-driven market approach. Rather than competing with massive studios for a general gaming audience, every level of the Strategy Map is aligned toward serving the specific niche of horse enthusiasts.

At the bottom of the Strategy Map, the "Learning \& Growth" and "Internal Business Process" perspectives focus on cost-effective scalability and niche quality. By using AI tools (Means 20, 21, 23), outsourcing non-core tasks (Means 19), and investing in scalable infrastructure, anti-cheat systems, and beta testing (Means 34, 35, 36 supporting Goal 7), Neigh Corp keeps its operational costs under control despite a low development budget (Influencer 24) and strong supplier power (Influencers 23, 25, 26). This allows the internal development team to focus on specialised equestrian simulation mechanics (Means 27--33).

In the "Customer" perspective, these specialised internal processes translate into a differentiated product (Goal 2) that protects Neigh Corp against competitive rivalry, new entrants, and casual substitutes. Although showing pop-up ads (Means 22) is included as a supplementary revenue stream, Neigh Corp cannot rely heavily on them because intrusive advertisements alienate players and face strict legal and platform constraints (Influencers 5, 18, 19, 21). Instead, Neigh Corp primarily drives growth through a grassroots marketing strategy. By encouraging streamers and YouTubers (Means 3--6, 9), offering early access (Means 1), fostering clubs and community events (Means 24, 25), and partnering with real-life equestrian brands and competitions (Means 7, 8), the company builds an authentic brand (Goal 9) and an engaged community (Goals 3, 5, 6, 10) across the VDML network. Finally, at the top of the Strategy Map, this customer loyalty and niche differentiation weaken buyer power and convert directly into sustainable microtransactions (Goal 4) and recurring monthly subscriptions (Means 17, Goal 8), fulfilling the overarching "Financial" goal of profiting from Golden Meadows (Goal 1, Objective 6).

For that reason, licenses are also engaged in zero-to-many of these events, as they may or may not be used by many asset design use events.
    
        Finally, each event is engaged in a one-to-many relationship with the conversion process, as each of the events may need to occur multiple times to finalise a merchandise design. The events are converted into the \textit{Merch Design Acquisition} increment event, which produces the final \textbf{Merch Design} resource.

The Exchanges are all modelled in a similar way, with the main difference being added types for some economic resources (Hosting Type and Labour Type). We describe the Premium Pass Exchange as an example.


The Premium Pass Exchange describes how Neigh Corp provides premium game access to a player in return for money. Neigh Corp and the player are the economic agents, while Premium Game Access and Money are the resources exchanged.

The Premium Pass Order represents the agreement between the two parties. It records the order ID, creation date, status and total amount. The order includes two commitments: the Premium Pass Purchase Orderline and the Premium Pass Payment Orderline. These represent the promise to provide premium access and the promise to pay for it. The reciprocity relationship connects these commitments because each is made in return for the other.

The commitments are fulfilled through two economic events. The Premium Pass Assignment records the actual allocation of premium access to the player's account, including the account ID, activation date and status. The Premium Pass Payment records the payment received, including the payment ID, amount, payment method, transfer date and status.

From Neigh Corp's perspective, the assignment is a decrement event because access is provided to the player, while the payment is an increment event because money is received. The exchange duality relationship connects these events as the two sides of the exchange. The model therefore distinguishes between what the parties promise and what they actually provide.

\subsubsection{Sales Process}

\begin{center}
    \begin{figure}[H]
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,
                     height=0.85\textheight,
                     keepaspectratio]{figures/IS1_Project_EPC_Model-Sales-Process.pdf}
        \caption{Sales Pocess \textit{Neigh Corp}}
        \label{fig:Sales_process}
    \end{figure}
\end{center}

Involved in the Sales Process are the organisational units Marketing, Sales, Player, Billing, Payment Provider, Shop System, and the ERP System.
The sales process describes how Neigh Corp sells premium passes to players, from promoting the pass to completing the order and handling subscription renewal.

The process starts when new premium content is added to the game. Marketing promotes the premium pass, and Sales presents the pass and its prices when the store is opened. The player can either select a premium pass or leave without buying. If the player leaves, Marketing sends a follow-up offer. If the player selects a pass, they can apply a promotional code, a bundle offer, or continue without either option. The final price is then determined.

Sales checks whether the player is eligible to purchase the pass. If the player is not eligible, the Shop System shows both the reason and an alternative, and the sale is not completed. If the player is eligible, they confirm the purchase, and Sales confirms the order.

After the order is confirmed, Billing creates an invoice while the Payment Provider processes the payment. Both the invoice and a confirmed payment are required before the Game System assigns the purchased items to the player's account. Billing then sends a receipt and confirmation, and Sales marks the order as completed.

If the payment fails, the Payment System increases a failure counter. After the first failed attempt, the player receives an alternative payment link that is valid for two days. If the player pays through this link, the process returns to payment processing. If the link expires or the payment fails more than once, the order is marked as failed and the invoice is cancelled. Support then contacts the player about the failed order.

For completed orders, Sales requests player feedback and analyses the sales results, whether or not feedback is received. The ERP System then checks the subscription status. If the subscription is renewed after one month, a new order enters the order-handling process. If the subscription is cancelled, Sales records the reason for cancellation and the sales cycle ends.


\subsubsection{Procurement Process}
\begin{center}
    \begin{figure}[H]
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,
                     height=0.85\textheight,
                     keepaspectratio]{figures/IS1_Project_EPC_Model-Procurement-Process.pdf}
        \caption{Procurement Processes \textit{Neigh Corp}}
        \label{fig:Procurement_process}
    \end{figure}
\end{center}

In the Procurement Process, the organisational units Procurement, ERP System, Manager, Office, and Finance are involved.

The process starts when the company identifies a need to purchase goods or services. The Procurement department creates an internal purchase request, which is then registered. The ERP system checks the value of the request. If the value exceeds SEK 50,000, a manager reviews the request and either approves or rejects it. Requests of SEK 50,000 or less continue without managerial approval.
Approved requests and requests that do not require approval are released for purchasing. The Procurement department then selects a supplier. If the manager rejects the request, the ERP system notifies the requester and the case is closed.

After the supplier is selected, the Procurement department creates a purchase order and negotiates the payment terms with the supplier. If the terms are agreed without prepayment, the order is sent to the supplier. If the supplier requires prepayment, a manager must approve it. If approved, the Finance department pays the prepayment, and Procurement then sends the purchase order to the supplier. If the manager rejects the prepayment, Procurement cancels the purchase order and the process ends.

After the purchase order is sent, the Office receives the goods or services and registers the goods receipt. In parallel, the ERP system receives the invoice. Both steps must be completed before the process continues. The Finance department then compares the purchase order, the goods receipt and the invoice through a three-way match. If they match, Finance settles the invoice. If they do not match, the Procurement department rejects the invoice and notifies the supplier.

\subsubsection{Hiring Process}
\begin{center}
    \begin{figure}[H]
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,
                     height=0.85\textheight,
                     keepaspectratio]{figures/IS1_Project_EPC_Model-Hiring-Process.pdf}
        \caption{Hiring Process \textit{Neigh Corp}}
        \label{fig:Hiring_process}
    \end{figure}
\end{center}

In the Hiring Process, the involved organisational units are HR, Manager, and IT.

When a need for staff is identified, the Hiring Process begins. A manager creates a staffing request which is being reviewed by HR. If they do not approve, the manager is notified and the request will be closed. If they approve, they create a job posting with the manager that they publish. Then, the applications will be collected and screened by HR. The manager shortlists the candidates and HR schedules interviews. The manager conducts interviews and evaluates the candidates. If there are no fitting candidates to select, HR closes the recruitment. If one candidate get selected, HR prepares a job offer, sends it to the candidate and HR and the manager wait for an answer. If the candidate declines the offer, the manager decides whether to close the recruitment if no other candidate should be selected, or if the job should be offered and sent out to another candidate.

When an offer is accepted by a candidate, HR prepares and sends out an employment contract. After the contract is signed, IT sets up IT access and sets up the workspace while the manager plans an introduction for the new employee.
Then, the new employee is welcomed on their first day by the manager. The process is completed now that the employee is onboarded.\\

\subsubsection{Beta Testing Process}
\begin{center}
    \begin{figure}[H]
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,
                     height=0.85\textheight,
                     keepaspectratio]{figures/IS1_Project_EPC_Model-Beta-Testing-Process.pdf}
        \caption{Beta Testing Process \textit{Neigh Corp}}
        \label{fig:Beta_Testing_Process}
    \end{figure}
\end{center}

In the Beta Testing Process consists of the Sub-Processes Closed Beta Testing and Open Beta Testing.
When the final Game Development Stage is reached, the Closed Beta Testing begins.
The management and developer team selects a group of 50 individuals that are supposed to play Golden Meadows for the first time. Individuals from the list are selected and receive invitations from the Management, that they agree or disagree to. If they disagree, another individual is asked. If the individual agrees, the Management proposes a contract and a NDA to them. If they don't sign them, someone else from the list is asked. If they agree and sign, the Management sends a game copy to the Closed Beta Tester and waits for their feedback for two month, as agreed in the contract. 

If feedback is received, the Closed Beta Tester is paid and the Management sends it to the developers. If no feedback is received after two months, the Management decides if a reminder should be sent to the Closted Beta Tester. If so, they will be notified and the Management waits another week. If no additional reminder should be sent, the Closed Beta Tester is simply notified of the cancellation of the process.

The developers evaluate the feedback and base new implementations and changes on them if needed. 
Now, the Closed Beta Testing is completed with making Golden Meadows available on the desired Game Vendors. The Open Beta Testing starts by publishing the game to 100.000 randomly selected Players who get early access to the game.
After five months, all their feedback from the Game Vendors and the Game Forum is collected and sent to the developers by the Management. The developers evaluate the feedback, implement final changes if needed and finally Golden Meadows is published for all Players.

\subsection{Description of KPIs}

We defined 8 Lagging and 3 Leading KPIs for monitoring our progress. All stand in relationship with the EAI that feeds the dashboard.
Each KPI is related to one or more company goals, specified with target values and mapped to the IT system.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Golden Meadows income break even} - Lagging KPI related to Goal 1 (To profit from Golden Meadows) as well as Objective 6 (One billion dollars annual income 2 years after the release, or reach break even within 2 years). The only identified target value is to break even by end of year two, the related IT solution being EAI and the data sources identified being ERP & CRM. The measured business process is Beta Testing Process Model.

\item \textbf{Time of attaining specific microtransaction count} - Lagging KPI concerning Goal 4 and Objective 3 (To get users to make payments inside of Golden Meadows and to receive 4000 daily microtransactions by the end of year 1). The target value is derived from the objective and is simply to attain more than or exactly 4000 microtransactions no longer than one year after release. The same IT data sources are used as in the previous KPI, as well as the same IT solution.

\item \textbf{Follower count on Steam pre-release}, also identified as a lagging KPI, related to Goal 3 (To gain a community that is interested in all of the company's games) and Objective 1 (Have 9.000 followers on Steam before the release). Target value: more than or exactly 9000 followers. ERP is the only data source while the IT solution is the same as in the previous two lagging KPI's.

\item \textbf{Amount of monthly subscriptions} - Lagging KPI related only to goal 8 (To generate recurring revenue from the player), sharing the same data sources and the IT solution as the first two identified KPI's. Target value is 200000, i.e. the amount of subscribers.

\item \textbf{Revenue generated by microtransactions monthly} - related goals 1 & 4 (To profit from Golden Meadows and To get users to make payments inside of Golden Meadows), also being lagging, having the target value of no less than 83 million USD. The same data sources and the IT solution are used as before.

\item \textbf{Maintain retention rate monthly} - Leading KPI related to the same goals as the previous KPI, only using CRM as its data source while also using EAI as the IT solution. Its target value is more than or equals to 90\%, representing retention rate. This KPI influences lagging KPI's related to revenue. 

\item \textbf{Number of scheduled beta tests} - Leading KPI influencing \textit{Time of Beta Testing Completion}, data sources being source code, documentation, as well as project mangement. The target value is no more than 100 test sessions. The measured business process is Beta Testing Process while the IT solution is EAI. It relates to goal 7 (to ensure the same quality of game mechanics for all) and objective 7 (complete beta testing 1 month before release).

\item \textbf{Change in Steam rating within last month} - Third leading KPI influencing \textit{Steam Follower Count}. Its target value is greater than mediocre/average rating, data source being ERP and IT solution being EAI. It relates to goal 2 (to make Golden Meadows enjoyable to play for the target market) as well as objective 8 (reach 4 out of 5 stars for Golden Meadows on Google Play 3 months after the release AND reach the "very positive" rating on steam within one year after release).

\item \textbf{Very high Steam rating within a year} - Lagging KPI related to the same goal and objective as the previous one, its target value being "highest rating", data source and IT solutions also being identical to the ones used by the leading KPI above.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Evaluation of KPIs}
The provided KPIs in the previous section are aligned with the evaluation Eckerson proposes in "Performance Dashboards: Measuring, Monitoring, and Managing Your Business".

The twelve evaluation points that are listed are aligned, owned, predictive, actionable, few, easy, balanced \& linked, trigger changes, standardized, context-driven, reinforced with incentives, and relevant.

The KPIs are designed to be aligned with the company's goals and objectives, with each KPI linked to specific business outcomes. They include both leading and lagging indicators, allowing the company to monitor factors that influence future performance as well as measure achieved results. The target values provide measurable performance expectations, while the identified business processes and IT systems indicate where the relevant data can be obtained and how it can be integrated into the dashboard.

Furthermore, the KPIs cover several areas of business performance, including financial results, customer retention, marketing performance, and beta testing. This supports a balanced view of the company's progress. The defined targets can also help identify deviations from expected performance and indicate when corrective action may be necessary.

However, not all evaluation points can be confirmed from the KPI definitions alone. Criteria such as clear ownership, ease of understanding, standardized measurement, contextual interpretation, and the use of incentives require additional information about how the KPIs will be implemented and managed. Therefore, while the proposed KPIs address several of Eckerson's evaluation principles, their full compliance with all twelve criteria depends on the company's broader performance management practices.
