\section{Introduction}
This section is to give a general introduction to the company, including a description of its
main product offerings, markets, and strategy.\\

Neigh Corp is a small game company aiming to create its first horse-themed game.
The product is an MMO with "gacha mechanics", a video game system in which the player spends in-game currency to receive a random in-game item, that combines various semi-realistic, real-life horse-related activities into a single game. The game is designed to be refreshing, immersive, and modern, and allows the player to roleplay as a horse trainer, enjoy riding in an open world, take part in competitions, and gather and sell horses and horse equipment. The game will have a lively community active in the game forum.\\
The desired customer base consists mostly of teenagers and young adults who are interested in horses. Neigh Corp welcomes experienced gamers as well as players who are completely new to gaming.\\
The primary sources of income are a recurring premium membership subscription that is offered to players, as well as in-game cosmetic purchases and microtransactions for in-game advantages.

Porter's Five Forces

The summary draws on Porter's \textit{Competitive Strategy}. Its starting
point is that profitability differs between industries, and that part of this
difference is explained by industry structure. Porter models this structure
as five competitive forces:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Rivalry among existing firms.} Rivalry is more intense with
    many similar-sized firms, slow market growth, high fixed costs, low
    switching costs, little product differentiation, high exit barriers, and
    a diversity of rivals. Firms compete through price, product
    differentiation, distribution channels, and supplier relationships.
  \item \textbf{Threat of substitutes.} Substitutes are products from
    \emph{other} industries that meet the same need (for example, glass or
    plastic versus aluminum cans). They make demand more elastic and limit
    the prices firms can charge.
  \item \textbf{Buyer power.} Buyers are powerful when they are concentrated,
    purchase large volumes, or can credibly integrate backward. They are weak
    when they are fragmented, face high switching costs, or when producers can
    integrate forward.
  \item \textbf{Supplier power.} This is the mirror image of buyer power.
    Suppliers are powerful when they are concentrated, hard to switch away
    from, or able to integrate forward.
  \item \textbf{Threat of new entrants.} Entry is limited by barriers such as
    government policy, patents and proprietary knowledge, asset specificity,
    economies of scale (minimum efficient scale), brand identity, capital
    requirements, and access to distribution. Exit barriers work similarly and
    worsen rivalry.
\end{enumerate}

The model describes an industry at a given point in time. Industries change
through innovation, so the forces shift over time. At the business-unit level,
firms respond with one of three generic strategies: \textbf{cost leadership},
\textbf{differentiation}, or \textbf{focus}.

Connecting the Five Forces to a Goal Model

Five forces analysis describes the external environment that a goal model
(e.g., iStar, GRL, or KAOS) otherwise leaves implicit. Consider a studio
running a live-service mobile game.

\subsection*{How this shows up in the model}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Actors and dependencies:} Players, platform holders,
    competitors, and suppliers become actors. The strength and replaceability
    of each dependency corresponds to buyer or supplier power.
  \item \textbf{Softgoals and contributions:} The forces become positive or
    negative contribution links to softgoals such as \emph{Profitability},
    \emph{Player loyalty}, and \emph{Market position}.
  \item \textbf{Obstacles and threats:} Rivalry, substitutes, and new
    entrants can be modeled as obstacles to top-level goals.
  \item \textbf{Generic strategies as top goals:} \emph{Differentiate through
    narrative and art style} or \emph{Focus on a niche} becomes a high-level
    goal, decomposed into subgoals and tasks (for example, \emph{Produce
    high-quality events} as an AND-decomposition).
\end{itemize}

Example fragment

\begin{itemize}[label=--]
  \item \textbf{Goal:} Sustainable profit (AND-decomposition)
  \begin{itemize}[label=--]
    \item \textbf{Softgoal:} Loyal player base --- hurt by high rivalry and
      low switching costs
    \begin{itemize}[label=--]
      \item \textbf{Task:} Run seasonal events and community features
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Softgoal:} Strong distribution --- constrained by platform
      power
    \begin{itemize}[label=--]
      \item \textbf{Task:} Add a direct sales channel (reduces supplier
        dependency)
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Softgoal:} Differentiation --- counters entrants and rivalry
    \begin{itemize}[label=--]
      \item \textbf{Task:} Invest in original IP and a unique art style
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

In short, the five forces explain \emph{why} certain goals matter and what
threatens them, while the goal model turns that analysis into concrete goals,
tasks, and dependencies that can be analyzed and traded off.
